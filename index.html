How Much Does a Website Cost in 2026? A No-Nonsense Guide

A clear breakdown of what a business website really costs in 2026, from $1,500 brochure sites to custom builds, and what drives the price.

By the ANCYA studio 14 October 2026 7 min read

“How much does a website cost?” is the question every business owner asks first — and the one most agencies answer with a fog of “it depends”. It does depend. But that doesn't mean you can't get a straight answer.

Here's what a website actually costs in 2026, what moves the number, and how to tell whether a quote is fair.

The short answer

For a credible small-business website, budget $1,500–$6,000 as a one-time cost. That covers a custom-designed brochure site with five to twelve pages, a mobile-first build, basic SEO and a contact form.

An established business that needs a content-managed site, a brand refresh and proper conversion work should expect $8,000–$25,000. E-commerce, web apps, integrations and multi-language builds start around $20,000 and climb from there.

At ANCYA, our packages start at $1,500 (Sprout), $4,900 (Grow) and custom (Scale). Those are real starting points, not bait-and-switch numbers.

What actually drives the price

1. Unique templates, not raw page count

Twenty blog posts sharing one template is less work than eight pages that each need a bespoke layout. Designers price complexity, not paper. A five-page site with a custom calculator can cost more than a forty-page catalogue.

2. Design depth

A template with your logo dropped in is cheap. A design system built around your brand — with real typographic hierarchy, custom illustration and states for every component — takes longer and shows.

3. Content

Copy is routinely 20–30% of a project. If you can supply clear, final text, you save money. If you need strategy, writing and editing, budget for it properly rather than treating it as an afterthought.

4. Functionality

A CMS, booking, payments, CRM sync, member areas, multi-language — each adds build and testing time. Integrations are where “simple” projects quietly become complex.

5. Who does the work

A solo freelancer, a small studio and a large agency have different overheads. None is automatically better. What matters is who actually does the work, how senior they are, and whether you can talk to them directly.

Three realistic budget bands

BudgetWhat it buys
$1,500–$4,000A focused brochure site, 5–8 pages, custom over a simple structure, light or no CMS, core SEO settings.
$5,000–$15,000Custom design for an established business, 10–20 pages, CMS, SEO foundations, analytics and a brand refresh.
$20,000+E-commerce, web apps, complex integrations, content strategy, motion, accessibility audits and ongoing iteration.

Where cheap sites get expensive

A $700 site is not a bargain if you rebuild it in eighteen months. The hidden costs show up later: slow pages that lose mobile visitors, a CMS nobody can use, missing SEO fundamentals, and a design that makes a good business look small.

The cheapest quote is rarely the lowest total cost. Ask what's included after launch — and what it will cost to change your mind.

How we price at ANCYA

Every project gets a fixed written quote after a short discovery call. We take a deposit to start, then milestone payments. No hourly surprises, no invoice for answering an email. If we underestimated the work, that's our problem to solve.

Five questions to ask before you sign

  • Who will actually design and build this — and can I meet them?
  • What exactly is included, and what is explicitly not?
  • Who owns the code, content and accounts when we're done?
  • What happens after launch, and what does support cost?
  • Can I speak to a client whose project resembled mine?

If an agency can answer those five clearly, you're already in a better position than most buyers.

Most websites don't fail loudly. They don't crash or disappear. They quietly lose a few enquiries a week — a slow page here, a buried phone number there — until the owner wonders why the pipeline feels thinner than it used to.

Here are twelve signs worth checking this week. Be honest with yourself; the fixes are often cheaper than the leak.

The twelve warning signs

1. It takes more than three seconds to load

Every extra second costs attention. Test your homepage on a phone using a throttled connection, not just your office Wi-Fi.

2. It's awkward on a phone

Tiny text, sideways scrolling, buttons too close together. If you have to pinch and zoom, so does your customer — and they won't.

3. The headline describes what you do, not why it matters

“We provide integrated solutions” is not a promise. Say what changes for the customer, in their words.

4. Contact details are hidden

If your phone number and email are only in the footer, you're making people hunt. Put a clear route to a human on every page.

5. The design looks older than your business

Visitors use visual quality as a proxy for competence. A dated site quietly tells them you've stopped paying attention.

6. There are broken links or dead ends

A 404 on a key page, a form that submits nowhere, a social icon pointing to the platform's homepage. Small errors, expensive message.

7. There's no proof

Testimonials, case studies, reviews, client logos, real numbers. Claims are cheap; evidence is persuasive.

8. You can't update it without a developer

If changing a price requires an email and a wait, the site will drift out of date. That drift is visible to customers.

9. You don't show up for your own category

Search for what you sell plus your town. If competitors fill the page and you don't, you're invisible at the exact moment of intent.

10. The navigation is a maze

Too many top-level items, clever labels nobody understands, important pages buried three levels deep. Clarity beats cleverness.

11. The call to action is vague

“Submit” and “Learn more” ask nothing and promise nothing. “Get a quote in one business day” tells the visitor what happens next.

12. The form asks for too much too soon

Every optional field reduces completion. Ask for the minimum needed to start a conversation, then qualify later.

What to fix first

Don't redesign everything at once. Fix in order of return: clarity first (headline, offer, primary action), then speed and mobile, then proof, then search visibility.

Those four changes often recover more enquiries than a full visual refresh — and they're far cheaper to test.

If several of these signs sound familiar, a short audit is usually enough to decide whether to repair or rebuild. Either is fine; drifting is the expensive option.

Somewhere along the way, “mobile-friendly” became a box to tick: the desktop site, squeezed until nothing technically overflows. That's not mobile-first. It's desktop-first with a hangover.

Mobile-first means designing for the smallest screen and the slowest connection first, then expanding to larger screens. It changes what you prioritise, not just how things wrap.

Why it matters more in 2026

For most small businesses, the majority of traffic now arrives on a phone — often from search, social or a link shared in a message. Google also indexes the mobile version of your site, so what a phone sees is effectively what search sees.

And the phone is where decisions happen: the commute, the queue, the sofa. If the experience is clumsy, the visitor doesn't wait for the desktop version. They choose someone else.

What mobile-first actually changes

Content priority

On a phone, only the first screen or two gets read. That forces a useful question: what are the three things a visitor must understand? Everything else is secondary.

Tap targets

Buttons and links need at least 44×44 pixels of touch area, with space between them. Thumb-friendly layout is not a luxury.

Performance budget

Start with a limit — say, under 1.5 seconds to largest contentful paint on a mid-range phone — and design within it. Images, fonts and scripts all compete for that budget.

Forms

Fewer fields, correct input types, sensible autocomplete. On a phone keyboard, every unnecessary field feels like a tax.

Navigation

A short, obvious menu. Persistent access to the primary action. No hover-dependent interactions, because thumbs don't hover.

The three-second phone test

Open your site on a phone, on cellular data, as a first-time visitor. Can you tell what the business does, who it's for and what to do next within three seconds? If not, the mobile design isn't finished.

Common mistakes

  • Shrinking the desktop layout instead of reprioritising it.
  • Hiding the primary call to action inside a hamburger menu.
  • Body text below 16px, or low-contrast grey on white.
  • Carousels and hover effects that don't work with a thumb.
  • Huge hero images that take seconds to appear.
  • Forms with tiny fields that trigger zoom on iOS.

What good looks like

A clear headline, a readable subhead, one obvious action, fast images and a contact route that's never more than one tap away. It should feel calm — not like a desktop page holding its breath.

Design for the phone first and the desktop version becomes simpler, faster and clearer too. That's the quiet bonus of doing it in the right order.

A redesign is one of the larger cheques a small business writes for marketing. It deserves the same scrutiny as hiring or a new location: what will this actually return, and how will we know?

Here's a practical way to decide — and a realistic picture of what happens after you say yes.

When a redesign is worth it

You've outgrown the site's structure

New services, new audiences, new locations. When every addition requires a workaround, the structure is costing you more than a rebuild would.

Conversion is falling while traffic is stable

People still arrive; fewer of them act. That's usually a clarity, trust or friction problem — the kind a focused redesign can fix.

You can't update it

If simple changes need a developer, the site will fall out of date. That's a slow, guaranteed loss.

It's slow or insecure

Performance and security are not cosmetic. A site that fails either is actively costing you money.

Your brand has moved on

New positioning, new look, new tone. The website is where the gap between “who we are” and “how we appear” is most visible.

When a redesign isn't the answer

If the problem is traffic, fix marketing. If the problem is the offer, fix the offer. If the site was rebuilt last year and performs well, fix the specific pages that underperform instead. A redesign is a tool, not a cure-all.

How to estimate the return

Use a simple, honest formula:

Extra monthly enquiries × close rate × average customer value = monthly gain

Then compare that gain with the project cost to get a payback period.

Illustrative example: a $9,000 redesign produces three additional enquiries a month. You close 25% of them, and an average customer is worth $4,000. That's 3 × 0.25 × $4,000 = $3,000 a month, so the project pays for itself in roughly three months. Your numbers will differ — use your own, not ours.

If the payback period is longer than eighteen months, either the scope is too large or the conversion assumptions are too optimistic. Test them before committing.

What to expect during the process

A typical small-business redesign takes six to ten weeks: discovery, wireframes, visual design, build, content migration, QA and launch. Your main jobs are decisions and content — the two things that most often delay a project.

Make sure SEO is treated as part of the build, not an afterthought. Redirects, preserved URLs, metadata and structured data all need owners. A beautiful redesign that loses its rankings is an expensive way to start again.

Metrics to watch afterwards

  • Conversion rate by key page and device
  • Qualified enquiries — not just form submissions
  • Organic traffic and rankings for priority terms
  • Core Web Vitals and real-device load time
  • Engagement on the pages that precede a sale

Give it 60–90 days before judging. Then compare like with like, and keep iterating. The launch is the start of the return, not the whole of it.

A homepage has one job: help the right visitor decide to stay, and then decide to act. Most homepages fail because they try to serve everyone at once — the founder, the investor, the job seeker, the existing customer and the new lead — in one scrolling wall of everything.

Here's the anatomy of a homepage that converts, in the order visitors actually read it.

1. A clear promise above the fold

The first screen must answer three questions: what is this, who is it for, and why should I care? The most reliable headline formula is outcome + audience + differentiator.

For example: “Bookkeeping for design studios that hate surprises.” It names the service, the audience and a point of view. Your subhead can add the proof or the mechanism.

Skip the poetry. Clarity converts; cleverness is a bonus, not a foundation.

2. One primary action

Pick the single most valuable next step — book a call, request a quote, start a trial — and make it visually dominant. A secondary action is fine, but it should be quieter. When everything is emphasised, nothing is.

3. Proof, early

Trust is built in seconds. Put one strong piece of evidence near the top: a specific testimonial, a recognisable client logo, a result, a review score. “Great service!” is noise. “They cut our quote turnaround from three days to four hours” is proof.

4. The problem in the visitor's words

Before you explain your solution, show that you understand the problem. A short section naming the frustration — in language the customer would actually use — creates the feeling of being understood. That feeling is the beginning of trust.

5. Services in plain language

Three to six cards, each leading with an outcome rather than a service label. “Get found by people already searching” beats “SEO services”. Keep the copy short enough to scan on a phone, and link each card to a fuller page.

6. How it works

Reduce uncertainty by showing the path: what happens first, what you need from them, how long it takes. Three or four steps is enough. This section quietly answers the question “what am I getting myself into?”

7. Objection handling

Price, timeline, risk, commitment. A short FAQ or a transparent pricing note removes friction before it becomes a reason to leave. If you can say “projects start at $X” or “you can cancel any time”, say it.

8. A human face

People buy from people. A real photo (or a considered illustration), a name, a short story and a direct contact route make the business feel like a place with humans in it — not a lead-generation machine.

9. A final, low-friction call to action

Repeat the primary action at the end, but lower the perceived risk: “Tell us what you're working on. We'll reply within one business day — no pitch.” Make the button specific. Reduce the number of fields.

What to remove

  • Auto-rotating carousels nobody controls.
  • Generic stock photography of handshakes.
  • A mission statement before a customer promise.
  • Five competing calls to action.
  • Autoplay video with sound, or heavy animation that delays the message.
  • “Welcome to our website.”

The five-second test

Show your homepage to someone unfamiliar with your business for five seconds, then hide it. Ask: what do they do, who is it for, and what would you do next? If they can't answer, the page isn't finished — regardless of how it looks.

A homepage doesn't need to say everything. It needs to say the right thing, clearly enough that the right person wants to keep going.

Choosing a web design agency is part procurement, part chemistry. The work is invisible until it's done, the stakes are high, and everyone claims to be “strategic” and “results-driven”.

The good news: a few direct questions separate a reliable partner from an expensive lesson.

Start with the work, not the pitch

Look for case studies that resemble your situation — similar size, similar complexity, similar audience. Then ask what actually changed after launch. A real agency will have numbers, or at least a clear before-and-after story. A weak one will show you a gallery and change the subject.

Beware the portfolio that is entirely big-brand work when you're a ten-person business. You may be hiring a sales team, not the team that made those sites.

Six questions that reveal everything

1. Who will actually do the work?

Ask for names and roles. If the people in the pitch disappear after signing, the relationship is being sold by one team and delivered by another. You want to meet the designer and developer.

2. What happens after launch?

Support, training, CMS handover, care plans, costs. A site without a post-launch plan becomes a liability within months.

3. How do revisions work?

How many rounds are included? What counts as a round? What happens if you want to change direction? Get the answer in writing.

4. Who owns the code and the accounts?

You should own the domain, hosting, analytics, repository and content. If the agency keeps control, you don't have a website — you have a subscription with extra steps.

5. What isn't included?

The exclusions list matters as much as the inclusions. Copywriting, photography, stock licences, third-party fees, maintenance and SEO are common gaps.

6. Can I speak to a current client?

A confident agency will connect you. A hesitant one is telling you something.

Read the contract for five things

  • Scope: deliverables, page count, revision rounds and assumptions.
  • Payment schedule: deposits, milestones and what happens if the project pauses.
  • Intellectual property: when ownership transfers — ideally on final payment.
  • Timeline and dependencies: what they need from you, and what counts as a delay.
  • Termination: notice periods, kill fees and handover obligations.

Red flags worth taking seriously

  • A price far below every other quote, with no explanation.
  • No discovery phase — a quote before they understand your business.
  • Vague scope (“we'll design a great site”) and no deliverables list.
  • No written contract, or pressure to pay before you've read one.
  • Unwillingness to name the people doing the work.
  • Answers that dismiss your questions as “technical details”.
  • High-pressure deadlines that exist only to close the sale.

What a good process looks like

Discovery before quoting. A fixed written scope. Weekly demos on a staging link. Clear feedback windows. A launch checklist. Handover documentation and a training session. A named contact who answers questions without a three-day delay.

None of that is exotic. It's just disciplined — and discipline is a better predictor of a good outcome than a beautiful pitch deck.

Trust your gut on communication

You'll spend weeks with this team. Do they answer directly? Do they say “I don't know, I'll find out”? Do they push back when you ask for something unwise? The agency that tells you the truth in the sales process is the one more likely to tell you the truth during the build.

Choose the team that makes you feel informed, not impressed. The website will be better for it.

A cookie is a small text file a website stores in your browser. It can remember things between pages and visits — for example, whether you've dismissed a banner. Similar technologies include localStorage, which stores preferences on your device without sending them with every request.

Cookies aren't programs and can't access other files on your computer.
